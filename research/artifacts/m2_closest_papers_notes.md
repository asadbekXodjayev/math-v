# M2 — Closest-paper fact sheets (verbatim-quote extraction)

Method: full text fetched on 2026-10-17 from arXiv HTML (papers from Dec 2023 on: arxiv.org/html/<ID>) or ar5iv (older papers: ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/<ID>), stripped to plain text, and read section by section plus targeted keyword search (seed, overlap, position, tokeniz*, memoriz*, limitation, etc.). Title/authors/dates/comments verified from export.arxiv.org API (`id_list=` query). Quotes are verbatim from the fetched text (LaTeX alt-text rendered as in the HTML, e.g. `10^{-8}`); "[…]" marks an elision. Location = section / table / figure / appendix as labelled in the HTML.

Conventions: "not stated" = I searched the full text (headings + keyword grep + reading the relevant sections) and found no statement. Figure contents (curves, error bands) are NOT visible in the text extraction — only captions/prose — so claims about figures are limited to what captions/prose say.

Caveat on versions: ar5iv renders a specific arXiv version (usually the latest at render time); arxiv.org/html renders the latest HTML version. Version rendered is noted per paper where it could be determined.

Quote verification: an automated pass (scratchpad/scripts/verify_quotes.py) checked every double-quoted string of ≥25 characters in each section against that paper's extracted text (whitespace/quote-mark/dash normalised; "[…]" segments checked separately). Result: all paper quotes found verbatim; the only non-matches are (i) arXiv metadata comment fields (TMLR / ICML tutorial / EMNLP strings, sourced from the arXiv API, not the paper body) and (ii) the caller's own description of 2409.17391. Some quotes contain words the paper sets in curly double quotes, rendered here as 'single' quotes. Extraction note: a first HTML-to-text pass dropped text following "<" inside math alt-text; all papers were re-fetched with a fixed extractor before verification.

## 1. 2307.03381 — Lee, Sreenivasan, Lee, Lee, Papailiopoulos, "Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers"

- **Title/authors (arXiv API):** "Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers" — Nayoung Lee, Kartik Sreenivasan, Jason D. Lee, Kangwook Lee, Dimitris Papailiopoulos. Submitted 2023-07-07 (only v1 listed by the API). arXiv comment field: none. **Venue: not stated in arXiv metadata or in the fetched (ar5iv, v1) text.** (Widely cited as ICLR 2024 — NOT verified from the paper text.)
- **Model / size / init:** "NanoGPT features a decoder-only transformer architecture with six self-attention layers, six heads, and an embedding dimension of 384 , resulting in approximately 10.6 million parameters." (§3 Model and Data). "We train the NanoGPT model from random initialization, which we refer to as training from 'scratch'" (§3). GPT-2: Table 12 (App. C.2) lists 12 layers / 12 heads / 768 dim; §10 calls it "a larger GPT-2 model with 85 million parameters" while §1 says "scaling up to 124 million parameters" (internal inconsistency in the paper). GPT-2 both from scratch and fine-tuned from pretrained; GPT-3 (Ada/Curie/Davinci) "we exclusively use supervised fine-tuning on a pretrained model" (§3).
- **Number representation / tokenization:** "Unless stated otherwise, we use character-level tokenization and absolute position encoding." (§3). Vocabulary of 13 for plain addition: "a vocabulary size of 13, which only includes 10 numerals and 3 symbols" (§9). **Tokenization was an experimental variable only in the GPT-2 experiment (§10, Fig. 17):** arms = character-level vs. OpenAI Tiktoken BPE (vocab 50,257), with vs. without spaces between digits, from-scratch vs. pretrained. Quote: "GPT-2 demonstrates high performance in addition tasks with both character-level tokenization and Tiktoken with spaces between digits." (§10). Fig. 17 caption: "The results highlight the significance of utilizing pretrained models and incorporating spaces for consistent tokenization of numbers". (No per-arm numbers in prose; numeric values only in the figure.) The main experimental variable is **output formatting**, not tokenization: Plain / Reverse / Simplified Scratchpad / Detailed Scratchpad (Fig. 1, §3).
- **Positional encoding:** "We use a learnable absolute positional embedding initialized randomly, following the GPT-2 model." (App. C.2). **Not varied.** Relative PE mentioned only in Limitations as a remedy proposed by others.
- **Tasks / data generation:** addition (main), subtraction, multiplication (up to 2-digit), sine and sqrt (4-decimal precision) (§8.1); joint training on all five (§8.2); mixing with Shakespeare text (§9). Balanced sampling: "we aim to (i) balance digits […] and (ii) balance carry-ons" (§3 Structured Data Sampling); 10k-sample 3-digit set = "all 100 1-digit additions and a random sampling of 900 2-digit additions […] and 9,000 3-digit additions" with rejection sampling for carries (App. C.1.1).
- **Train/test split:** Instance-level exclusion is explicit: "The test dataset {\mathcal{D}}_{\text{test}} is constructed by randomly sampling pairs of operands not included in {\mathcal{D}}_{\text{train}} ." (§3); "For the test dataset, we uniformly sample 10,000 addition examples that do not overlap with the train dataset." (App. C.1.1); "For the test dataset, we sample 10,000 data points ( 7,000 for multiplication) that do not overlap with the train dataset." (App. C.1.1). GPT-3: "Performance evaluation was carried out on 1,000 examples that were disjoint from the training dataset." (§10). Note: for 3-digit addition the train set is not a memorizable fraction of the space but the train fraction is large for 1-digit (all 100 1-digit sums are in train).
  - Held-out operands/digits: "we randomly choose 100/200/500 numbers and exclude them from the training data" → Table 1: e.g. reverse "Exclusion Accuracy" 100.00% / 99.95% / 100% (§5.2). Excluding digit "5" at a position (Table 2): worst case reverse, LSB excluded, 66% on those samples.
  - Length generalization (§12): trained on 1- and 3-digit (no 2-digit) → "the NanoGPT model is incapable of performing 2-digit and 4-digit additions"; trained up to 7-digit plain → "the model is unable to generalize to a greater number of digits beyond what it has been trained on"; 10-digit binary → fails on 11-digit.
- **Seeds / variance:** **not stated** (no mention of random seeds or repeated training runs). Only variance reported is over prompt exemplars: "Standard deviations of these prompts are denoted by shaded areas in the plots." (§9).
- **Key quantitative findings:**
  - (a) Tokenization: GPT-2 char-level and Tiktoken-with-spaces both perform well; the Fig. 17 caption stresses spaces "for consistent tokenization" (i.e. BPE without spaces is worse). Only qualitative in prose; no per-arm numbers in text; no seeds.
  - (b) Memorization vs generalization / OOD: in-length generalization to unseen operand pairs and even excluded operands (Table 1); no length generalization (§12). Conclusion: "we find that while the model generalizes to unseen examples of the same number of digits, the problem of length generalization is quite difficult." (§14). Intro: "the models learn arithmetic not as a flexible algorithm, but more as a mapping function constrained to trained digit lengths. While this surpasses mere memorization, it falls short of comprehensive arithmetic 'understanding'." (§1).
  - (c) Data efficiency: plain "plateaus at slightly over 85\% even with 10,000 samples"; reverse shows "a notable phase transition between 1000 and 4000 samples" (§4.2). "Simplified Scratchpad data achieves 100\% accuracy with only 2000 samples, whereas the Reverse format requires more than double the number of samples. Furthermore, the Detailed Scratchpad format […] achieves perfect addition with just 1000 samples." (§6.1). Phase transition linked to rank-2 low-rank matrix completion: "both NanoGPT and LRMC exhibit a similar phase transition at approximately 1500 samples" (2-digit, §5.1).
  - (d) Architecture: only scale (NanoGPT 10.6M vs GPT-2) — "the larger model outperforms in both plain and detailed scratchpad evaluations" (§10). No RoPE/GLU/PE ablation.
  - (e) Output ordering / scratchpad: reverse (LSB-first) output is central (Lemma 1/2, §4); token cost: mean tokens per 3-digit example "13 for plain format, 15 for reverse format, 64 for simplified scratchpad format, and 281 for detailed scratchpad format" and "reverse formatting is the most token-efficient approach" (§11). Joint training (Table 4): e.g. × zero-shot 4.58 (individual) vs 14.28 (joint).
- **Stated limitations (§13):** "we did not observe any instances where the model could predict beyond the number of digits it had been trained on"; "Our model was limited to a GPT-type decoder-only architecture, primarily focusing on character-level tokenization. Although we have obtained some preliminary results on scaling up and incorporating BPE-based tokenization, it remains uncertain if all our findings can be generalized to the scale of LLMs being used in practice today." Also §8: "our focus is primarily on integer arithmetic for binary operations, considering a limited length of digits."

## 2. 1904.01557 — Saxton, Grefenstette, Hill, Kohli, "Analysing Mathematical Reasoning Abilities of Neural Models"

- **Title/authors (arXiv API):** "Analysing Mathematical Reasoning Abilities of Neural Models" — David Saxton, Edward Grefenstette, Felix Hill, Pushmeet Kohli (DeepMind). Submitted 2019-04-02 (v1 only). arXiv comment: none. **Venue: not stated in metadata or fetched text** (commonly cited as ICLR 2019 — not verified from text).
- **Models / size / init:** all trained from scratch on the dataset (no pretraining mentioned). Transformer (encoder–decoder): "We use an embedding size of d_{\text{model}}=512 , with h=8 attentional heads […] Each layer has an intermediate representation with dimension d_{\text{ff}}=2048 ." (§3.2). **Number of layers: not stated.** Parameter counts (Fig. 3 table): Simple LSTM 18M, Simple RMC 38M, Attentional LSTM (LSTM enc) 24M, Attentional LSTM (bidir enc) 26M, Attentional RMC 39M, **Transformer 30M**. Training: "batch size of 1024 split across 8 NVIDIA P100 GPUs for 500k batches" (§4.1).
- **Number representation / tokenization:** character-level for everything: "here we instead treat the question and answer as a sequence of characters, since we need to be able to embed arbitrary mathematical expressions." (§3.2); "a common alphabet of size 95" (§2.5). **Tokenization not varied.**
- **Positional encoding:** **not stated** (standard Transformer of Vaswani et al. cited; no PE details or ablation).
- **Task families / generation:** procedurally generated modules in algebra, arithmetic, calculus, comparison, measurement, numbers, polynomials, probability (§2.2; App. B), with module composition (§2.1). Generation samples answer first then works backwards: "we first sample the answer, and then work backwards to generate the question" (§2.3).
- **Train/test split:** "Per module, we generate 2\times 10^{6} train questions, and 10^{5} test (interpolation) questions." Overlap is controlled **probabilistically, not by exact dedup**: "the generation code guarantees lower bounds on the probability of a given question appearing. (Post-generation hashing does not in general work, since the same question may occur with linguistic variation, although we use it in a few limited cases.) We generate test questions such that any particular question has a probability of at most 10^{-8} , thus guaranteeing that at most 10^{-8}\times 2\times 10^{6}=2\% of the test questions to have already appeared in the training data." (§2.3). Extrapolation tests: "problems involving larger numbers, more numbers, more compositions, and (for probability questions) larger samplers" (§2.3), e.g. `add_or_sub_big`, `mul_big`, `mixed_longer`, `sort_more` (App. B).
- **Seeds / variance:** **not stated** (single numbers per model; no seeds/CI).
- **Key findings:**
  - Interpolation / extrapolation averages (Fig. 3): Transformer 0.76 / 0.50; Simple LSTM 0.57 / 0.41; Attentional LSTM bidir 0.58 / 0.42.
  - (b) "Modules on which good extrapolation performance was obtained include rounding larger numbers than seen during training, comparing more numbers, and adding and subtracting larger numbers. However for example models completely failed to add together more numbers than seen during training" (§4.2). Mixed arithmetic ~50% vs ≥90% for add/sub-several: "This is evidence that the models do not learn to do any algebraic/algorithmic manipulation of values, and are instead learning relatively shallow tricks" (§4.2). 1+1+…+1 fails at n=7 (§4.2 Other insights).
  - (d) "The Transformer performs the same as or significantly better than recurrent models across nearly all modules." (§4.2). Thinking steps: "increasing the number of 'thinking' steps […] from 0 up to 16 increased the performance" (attentional LSTM).
  - Real exam: "the Transformer model got 14/40 questions correct" (§4.3).
  - (a), (c), (e): no tokenization, sample-size, or output-ordering ablation.
- **Stated limitations (§5):** "Some modules are largely unsolved (for example those requiring several intermediate calculations) […] and extrapolation performance is low."; "We have not addressed linguistic variation or complexity in this dataset."; robustness: "on one trained Transformer, the question “ Calculate 17 * 4. ” gave the correct answer 68 , but the same question without the final full stop gave 69" (§4.2).

## 3. 2112.01898 — Charton, "Linear algebra with transformers"

- **Title/authors (arXiv API):** "Linear algebra with transformers" — François Charton (Meta AI). v1 2021-12-03, v2 2022-11-08. arXiv comment: "Transactions in Machine Learning Research (TMLR), October 2022".
- **Model / size / init:** trained from scratch (supervised, data generated on the fly). "All models use the transformer architecture from Vaswani et al. 2017 : an encoder and a decoder connected by cross-attention. Models have 512 dimensions, 8 attention heads and up to 6 layers" (§3); "small sequence to sequence transformers (up to 6 layers, 10 to 50 million trainable parameters)" (§1). Asymmetric enc/dec depths explored (e.g. 6/1, 1/6); larger models (12/24 layers) in App. D.3; LSTM/GRU and universal transformers in App. E.
- **Number representation / tokenization — an explicit experimental variable (4 arms):** "I propose four encoding schemes for matrix coefficients (set in scientific notation with three significant digits): P10, P1000, B1999, and FP15." (§2.1). Table 1: P10 = 5 tokens/coef, vocab 210; P1000 = 3 tokens, vocab 1100; B1999 = 2 tokens, vocab 2000; FP15 = 1 token, vocab 30000. Asymmetric (FP15 encoder / P1000 decoder) also used.
- **Positional encoding:** not named and **not varied**. Parameter-count appendix counts "a positional embedding of w_{p} words (corresponding to the maximum sequence length)" (App. G) — i.e. a parameterized (learned-table) positional embedding is implied by the parameter formula; type not otherwise stated.
- **Tasks / generation:** nine problems: transposition, addition, matrix–vector, matrix multiplication, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, singular values, SVD, inversion (§2). "the training data is generated by sampling random input matrices […] and computing the output O with a linear algebra package (NumPy linalg). All coefficients […] rounded to three significant digits" (§2). Coefficients "uniformly distributed in [-A,A] (with A=10 )" (§2.2).
- **Train/test split:** test sets freshly sampled: "At the end of every epoch (300,000 examples), a random test set (10,000 examples) is generated" (§3). **No explicit train/test overlap check stated** (continuous random matrices make overlap unlikely, but the paper does not state a check). Correctness criterion: "In most problems, I check that P verifies \|P-O\|<\tau\|O\| ." with L1 norm (§3). OOD: §5 tests eigenvalue models on different eigenvalue distributions (Laplace, Gaussian, uniform, positive) and different coefficient std; size generalization discussed (fixed-size models "do not generalize to different dimensions", App. F.1).
- **Seeds / variance:** **not stated.**
- **Key findings:**
  - (a) Encoding effect: "high accuracy can only be achieved with the P10 or P1000 encoding" for matrix-vector multiplication (§4.3); "Our best results are achieved using the P1000 and FP15 encodings. For most problems, P10 is dominated by the more economical P1000, and B1999 never finds its use" (§7); "For the hardest problems, eigenvectors and inversion, asymmetric encodings, FP15 in the encoder and P1000 in the decoder, achieve the best results." (§7).
  - (b) Memorization: explicit discussion "Do the models memoize? […] Three observations lead me to believe that this is not the case here." (§7) — reasons: mixed-size training easier than fixed-size; retraining cheaper than scratch; OOD generalization of Laplace-trained models to positive-definite matrices. OOD: Wigner-trained eigenvalue models reach "high accuracy ( 96\% at 2\% tolerance) so long 0.6\sigma_{tr}<\sigma_{tst}<\sigma_{tr} . Out of this range, model accuracy drops: to 54\% for 0.4\sigma_{tr} , to 26\% for 1.1\sigma_{tr} , to 2\% for 1.3\sigma_{tr} and to 0\% for 0.2\sigma_{tr} ." On other eigenvalue distributions: "With \sigma_{tst}=\sigma_{tr} , the model achieves 26\% accuracy for Laplace, 25 for Gaussian, 19 for uniform, and 0 for positive." vs. "models trained on matrices with Laplace eigenvalues only, or a mixture […] achieve 95\% accuracy over all test sets" (§5). "transformers only generalize to a narrow neighborhood around their training distribution" (§5, about Wigner-trained models).
  - (c) Sample size (examples seen, online data): "It takes 360 million examples for our best models to reach 25\% accuracy on 10\times 10 matrices. As a comparison, 40 million examples are required to train 5\times 5 models to 99\% accuracy, and 60 million for 8 \times 8 models." (§4.4). Inversion: "8 -head models need 250 millions examples to train to 75\% accuracy" (§4.6).
  - (d) Architecture: asymmetric shallow encoder or decoder effective; "using 10 or 12 heads with dimension 512 […] improves model accuracy" for eigenvalue/inversion (§7). No PE/GLU ablation.
  - (e) Output ordering / scratchpad: not studied.
- **Stated limitations (§7):** "Most experiments feature dense matrices with 5 to 10 dimensions. […] the methods proposed in this paper could probably not scale beyond 50\times 50 matrices." Caveat (§1): "This paper does not advocate replacing existing linear algebra algorithms with transformer-based implementations."

Scale: "the gap between tokenization directions decreases when models are scaled" (Abstract).

Beyond the expense of this experiment (making it intractable in academic settings), a key question also becomes how to 'compute'-control. The better compression ratio of up-to-3 digit tokens means a token-controlled experiment would result in some models 'seeing' more data."

## 5. 2405.17399 — McLeish et al., "Transformers Can Do Arithmetic with the Right Embeddings"

- **Title/authors (arXiv API):** "Transformers Can Do Arithmetic with the Right Embeddings" — Sean McLeish, Arpit Bansal, Alex Stein, Neel Jain, John Kirchenbauer, Brian R. Bartoldson, Bhavya Kailkhura, Abhinav Bhatele, Jonas Geiping, Avi Schwarzschild, Tom Goldstein. v1 2024-05-27, v2 2024-12-23 (HTML read = v2). arXiv comment: none. **Venue: not stated as an acceptance**; the v2 HTML contains a "NeurIPS Paper Checklist" (so prepared for NeurIPS; commonly cited as NeurIPS 2024 — acceptance not stated in text).
- **Model / size / init:** decoder-only, trained from scratch: "We train decoder-only causal language models to solve addition problems." (§3); "language models trained from scratch" (§3). Default hyperparameters (Table 5, App. A.8.1): Hidden Size = 1024; Intermediate Size = 2048; Number of Attention Heads = 16; Activation Function = GELUglu; Normalization Type = Post (LayerNorm); Initialization = Deepnorm (table cells, not prose). Depth: 16 effective layers in the main addition models ("depth sixteen transformer (decoder only) models", Fig. 1 caption). Parameter counts (Table 3, with Abacus + input injection): 16x1 = 122M; 8x2 = 64M; 4x4 = 34M; 2x8 = 19M; 1x16 = 12M (layers-in-block x recurrences). Architectures: standard transformer, + input injection, looped (weight-tied) transformer. Compute cap: "limit each training run to 8 exaFLOP of compute (a single Nvidia RTXA4000 GPU for 24 hours)" (§3).
- **Number representation / tokenization:** "We use a character level tokenizer for all experiments and greedy decoding in all testing." (App. A.8). **Tokenization not varied.** Operands written LSB-first: "inputs are formatted least significant digit first, e.g. 98282+3859172=2787472" (§3). No zero padding.
- **Positional encoding — THE experimental variable:** arms = Abacus (new), FIRE, NoPE, RoPE, and combinations (Abacus+FIRE, Abacus+RoPE). Abacus: "simple learned positional embeddings that are used to encode positions within each span of numerical tokens" (§1); random offset "from U[1,k] […] the default value for k in this study is 100" (§3.1). "Although Abacus Embeddings are implicitly combined with NoPE (no positional embeddings) embeddings for all experiments seen so far" (§4.4). Activation (GELU-GLU) fixed, not varied.
- **Tasks / data generation:** addition (main), addition+subtraction mix, multiplication (up to 15 digits), sorting (arrays up to 10 numbers x 10 digits), bitwise OR (App. A.3). "all training sets have 20 million samples […] We sample data with replacement and we stratify the data, so that all length pairs (i,j) are equally sampled during training." (§3). Evaluation: "For evaluation we sample 100 samples for each pair of operand lengths evaluated." (App. A.2).
- **Train/test split:** the design is length-based (ID = up to max train length; OOD = longer but at most 100 digits; 100+ OOD = 100 to 160 digits, equal lengths) (§3). **No instance-level dedup / train-test overlap check is stated** (ID test pairs are sampled independently; overlap not discussed). The generalization claim is about length extrapolation.
- **Seeds / variance:** "Since our training pipeline produces fairly consistent results, we report the mean over three runs (rather than using a best-of-ten reporting scheme ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) )." (§3). Some figures use five models (App. A.5). **No error bars/CIs:** NeurIPS checklist answer [No]: "While we average over several trials, we do not report exact error bars."
- **Key findings:**
  - (d) PE / architecture: "Combining Abacus Embeddings and standard positional embeddings, we observe dramatic improvements in accuracy such that models trained with at most 20 digit operands can generalize to problems with 120 digit operands." (§1); "training on only 20 digit numbers with a single GPU for one day, we can reach state-of-the-art performance, achieving up to 99\% accuracy on 100 digit addition problems" (Abstract). "increasing from 92.9\% to 99.1\% in out of distribution accuracy, an 87\% reduction in error compared to using the embeddings with standard architectures alone" with input injection + looped transformers (§1). "a model with two recurrences ( 8\times 2 ) incurs half the error of the purely non-recurrent model ( 16\times 1 ) for OOD problems" (§3.2). RoPE: "Rotary Embeddings are weak for length generalization. We show that combining Abacus Embeddings with RoPE does, in fact, yield improvement in operand length generalization." (§4.4). Sorting (Table 1): All OOD (30x30) FIRE 3.73 / Abacus 2.65 / Abacus+FIRE 4.48.
  - (b) Generalization is length-OOD; instance-level memorization vs generalization is not examined.
  - (c) Data: fixed 20M samples per run; App. A.4 varies maximum operand size, not sample count: "increasing the size of the operands in the training set allows for better generalization above one hundred digits for all models."
  - (e) Output order fixed at reversed (LSB-first); not ablated (index hints compared in App. A.7.5).
  - (a) Tokenization: not studied.
- **Stated limitations (§5):** "There are some intrinsic limitations that accompany any study involving language model training from scratch under compute constraints. However, the primary point of relevance for this study is that although we show the compatibility of Abacus Embeddings with FIRE and RoPE embeddings, we do not actually explore any natural language tasks." Also: "As Abacus Embeddings are a variant of absolute positional embeddings, technically they cannot generalize beyond the relative positions seen during training." (§3.1).
